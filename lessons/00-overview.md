# Swell, from the storm to the beach

Here is the thing we are trying to explain.

Somewhere in the Southern Ocean a low-pressure system sits for two days and
blows forty knots over a thousand kilometres of open water. The sea underneath
it is a mess: ten metres high, chaotic, foaming, with no discernible period.
Nothing about it looks like a wave.

Five days later and eight thousand kilometres away, a beach in Indonesia
receives clean lines. They are a metre and a half high. They arrive with a
period of eighteen seconds, which is to say the crests are half a kilometre
apart. They are so orderly you could set a metronome to them. And they arrive
in sets: four or five waves, then two minutes of nothing, then four or five
more.

Between those two pictures the ocean has done something remarkable. It has
taken noise and returned signal. What happened?

The answer is dispersion, and it takes about ten lessons to say properly. Here
is the shape of it.

The wind puts energy into the water across a wide band of frequencies at once
(lessons 3 and 4). Those frequencies then leave the storm and cross the ocean
at *different speeds* — long waves faster, short waves slower, and exactly in
proportion to their period (lessons 1 and 2). Over eight thousand kilometres
that difference in speed becomes a difference in arrival time of days. The
frequencies get sorted, arriving one after another, longest first. That sorting
is the whole trick: it is a prism, and the ocean is the glass (lesson 5). Along
the way most of the energy spreads out and is lost, but remarkably little of it
is actually dissipated (lesson 6).

What arrives is therefore very narrow in frequency. And a narrow band of
frequencies, superposed, does not give you an even train of waves. It gives you
beats — an envelope that swells and dies. That is what a set is (lesson 7). At
higher steepness the groups start to feed themselves, and the statistics
develop a fat tail (lesson 8).

Then the water gets shallow. The waves slow down, bunch up, steepen, and swing
round to face the beach (lesson 9). And finally the crest outruns the wave and
the whole thing falls over, in one of four distinct ways depending on a single
dimensionless number (lesson 10).

Lesson 11 walks one storm through the whole chain with numbers attached.

## How to read this

Each lesson derives what it uses. You need calculus, some comfort with Fourier
transforms, and a willingness to look at a partial differential equation
without flinching. You need no oceanography at all — that is the point.

Every equation here also exists as code, in `swells/`, with the same names. The
figures are generated by `figures/make_figures.py`, so nothing in the plots is
decorative. And the whole chain runs interactively in `app/`, where you can
turn the dials yourself.

Where a formula is empirical — and a surprising number of the important ones
are — it says so, with the range over which it was fitted. Where the literature
disagrees with itself, which it does more than you would hope, that is said too.
There is one place, in lesson 4, where two of the most-cited results in the
field are flatly inconsistent with each other, and we have to choose.

## A number to keep in your pocket

A twenty-second swell travels at

$$c_g = \frac{gT}{4\pi} = \frac{9.81 \times 20}{4\pi} = 15.6\ \text{m/s} = 56\ \text{km/h}$$

so it crosses ten thousand kilometres in seven and a half days. If anything in
this project ever disagrees with that, the project is wrong.
