# Swells

## What this is

A self-taught course, with a working simulator attached, on how a storm at sea turns
into sets of waves at a beach.

The question driving the whole thing: a storm dumps energy into the ocean across a
broad band of frequencies, all mixed together. By the time that energy reaches a
coastline thousands of kilometres away it has sorted itself into clean, near-periodic
groups that arrive in sets. How? What sorts it? What sets the interval between sets?
And what decides whether the thing finally breaks as a gentle spilling mush or a
barrelling plunger?

Two deliverables, developed together:

1. **`lessons/`** — markdown lessons that derive the physics.
2. **`swells/` + `app/`** — a simulator. You set storm strength, fetch, duration,
   distance to shore, and bathymetry; you watch the swell arrive, disperse, group into
   sets, shoal, and break.

The lessons and the simulator are one artifact, not two. Every equation derived in a
lesson exists as a named function in the package, and every figure in a lesson is
generated by that code. If they ever disagree, that is a bug in both.

## Audience

The author, who is comfortable with calculus, ODEs, and reading a PDE without
flinching, but who has no background in physical oceanography. Nothing about fluid
mechanics may be assumed. Everything about undergraduate maths may be.

## Style

The model is Anthony Zee — *Quantum Field Theory in a Nutshell*, *Einstein Gravity in a
Nutshell*. Concretely:

- Conversational and direct. "We" and "you". Talk to the reader like a person you like.
- Concise. Zee's sentences are short. He gets to the point and trusts you to keep up.
- Physical intuition first, formalism second. Say what's going on, *then* write the
  equation that says it precisely.
- Simplest-thing-first. Two sine waves before a full spectrum. Flat bottom before real
  bathymetry. Get the toy model right and the general case is bookkeeping.
- Derive things. Don't say "it can be shown". Either show it, or say plainly that
  you're skipping the grind and point to where it's done.
- Humour is allowed when it arrives on its own. It is never to be manufactured.
- Brief historical asides where they illuminate — Snodgrass tracking swell across the
  entire Pacific, Munk's wartime surf forecasts. A sentence or two, not a biography.

Explicitly not wanted: "In this lesson we will explore…". Bulleted lists of
definitions. Bold-face scattered for emphasis. Emoji. Section summaries that restate
what was just said. Padding of any kind. If a paragraph can be deleted without loss,
delete it.

Prose over lists. Lists are for things that are genuinely a list.

## How the work is split

A research model drafts the physics and the code with a free hand — its own choice of
lesson structure, its own derivations, its own architecture. It is explicitly asked
what's missing from the chain below, because the chain below is one person's guess at
the syllabus and not a specification.

Claude Code then takes that draft and does the second pass: rewrite into the voice
above, verify the derivations, check the numbers, restructure where the shape is wrong,
and fill in whatever came back as a stub. Physics found in the draft that isn't
anticipated here is a win, not a deviation — absorb it and update this file.

## Physics scope

The chain as currently understood, to be revised as the material comes in:

Wind blows over water (Phillips resonance, Miles instability) → energy enters a
spectrum, shaped by fetch and duration (JONSWAP, Pierson–Moskowitz) → the storm is a
patch of energy in frequency-direction space → deep-water dispersion sorts it, because
long waves travel faster (c_g = c_p/2 = gT/4π) → the arrival spectrogram is a straight
line with slope g/4πD, which lets you read the storm's distance off a buoy record →
geometric spreading and directional narrowing attenuate it → a narrow-band signal at
the coast has a slowly-varying envelope, which is what a set *is* → shoaling and
refraction as it feels the bottom → breaking, with the Iribarren number deciding what
kind.

Set formation is the heart of it and deserves the most careful treatment: beats between
neighbouring frequencies, envelope statistics of a narrow-band Gaussian process, the
bandwidth parameter, expected run lengths, and an honest verdict on "sets of seven".

## Simulator contract

Inputs: wind speed U₁₀, fetch, storm duration, storm distance and bearing, beach slope
and approach angle. Optionally a second storm, so the reader can see two swells beat
against each other.

Outputs: the source spectrum; a spectrogram at the buoy with the dispersion line drawn
on it; a synthesised surface elevation time series; its envelope with sets marked;
wave-by-wave breaker heights and breaker type; and a plain-language surf report (H_s,
peak period, set interval, waves per set).

Python + numpy is the reference implementation and must stay readable as *mathematics* —
variable names follow the lessons, and each module carries a docstring pointing at the
lesson that derives it. The browser UI runs that same Python via Pyodide, so there is
one source of truth and no risk of a JS port silently drifting. If Pyodide proves
unworkable, a JS port is acceptable only with golden-value parity tests against Python.

## Quality bar

Physics correctness outranks everything, including style. Empirical formulas are
labelled as empirical, with their range of validity and a citation. Where the
literature disagrees, say so rather than picking one silently.

Every non-trivial result gets a test: analytic limits (deep and shallow water), known
values (the dispersion slope for a given distance), conservation laws (energy flux
through shoaling), and reproduction of published figures where possible.

Numbers should be sanity-checkable by hand. A 20-second swell travels at about 56 km/h,
so it crosses 10,000 km in roughly seven and a half days. If the code disagrees, the
code is wrong.

## Not this

Not a wave forecasting model, and no attempt to compete with WAVEWATCH III. Not a surf
spot database. Not a game or a pretty ocean renderer — visualisation exists to explain,
and every plot must earn its place by making something clearer.
